TT or TN: earthing systems made simple

20 October 20264 min read

Key points

  • The earthing system describes how the source neutral and the metal casings of appliances are connected to earth.
  • It decides what disconnects the supply when an insulation fault occurs: the RCD in TT; the circuit breaker or fuse in TN, provided the fault current is high enough.
  • In a temporary installation, 30 mA RCDs are mandatory in France whatever the earthing system, and the neutral (N) stays separate from the protective conductor (PE): no PEN in flexible cables.

Electrical equipment often has a metal casing: an exposed-conductive-part. If a live conductor touches it, through damaged insulation for example, it becomes live: this is an insulation fault, and anyone touching it risks an electric shock. The earthing system describes the path of the fault current, and therefore which device will disconnect the supply, or not.

Two letters say it all

In French, it is called the “régime de neutre”. It is written with two letters:

  • The first concerns the neutral of the source, a transformer or a generator: T, connected directly to earth; I, isolated from earth or connected through a high impedance.
  • The second concerns the metal casings of appliances: T, connected to an earth electrode; N, connected to the source neutral by the protective conductor (PE), the green and yellow conductor.

A third system, IT, with an isolated neutral, is used where an interruption would be serious, such as operating theatres; we do not use it at events.

TT: the earth carries the current back, the RCD trips

The source neutral is connected to one earth electrode, the casings to another. The fault current returns through the ground, which limits it: it is usually too small to operate a circuit breaker or a fuse in time, which does not make it harmless. It is a residual current device (RCD), together with the earthing of the casings, that disconnects. In France, this is the system used for installations connected to the public low-voltage network.

TN: the fault becomes a short circuit, the breaker trips

The casings are connected to the neutral by the protective conductor. A fault of negligible impedance becomes a short circuit, which closes through this conductor: the circuit breaker (MCB) or fuse must disconnect within the time prescribed by the standard. Provided the current is high enough: it depends on the source and on the length and cross-section of the conductors, and is checked by calculation, then by measurement. If not, cables or protective devices are adapted, or, in TN-S, an RCD is added.

  • TN-S: neutral and protective conductor separate.
  • TN-C: combined in a single conductor, the PEN, only allowed in fixed installations, from 10 mm² in copper (16 mm² in aluminium), forbidden in locations with a fire or explosion risk, and incompatible with an RCD.

And at an event?

An event installation is temporary: according to the French national safety institute (INRS), 30 mA RCDs are mandatory there, as on all socket-outlets up to 32 A, whatever the earthing system.

On a venue supply, the event in principle fits into the earthing system that supplies it: usually TT in France if the venue is connected to the public low-voltage network. A large venue with its own transformer may be TN, sometimes TN-C on the large cables: the PEN is then split into neutral and PE before the event's distribution boards, which are protected by RCDs. In French exhibition halls, the fire safety regulations require the earth terminal of each stand board to be connected to the building's protective network, and forbid individual earth rods. Local rules differ: in the UK, for example, BS 7671 restricts the use of a PME (TN-C-S) earth for temporary installations.

Changing the earthing system requires a new source: a transformer with separate windings, or a generator. On a generator, earthing system, earthing arrangements and protective devices are chosen at the design stage and verified on site. A generator delivers far less short-circuit current than a transformer: in TN, disconnection by circuit breaker must be checked carefully, otherwise RCDs take over.

Finally, flexible cables cannot be used as a PEN: neutral (N) and protective conductor (PE) stay separate. Where the earthing system includes a neutral-earth link, there is only one, at the source or at the origin of the installation, never in downstream boards and boxes.

TT or TN: ask yourself what disconnects. In TT, the RCD; in TN, the circuit breaker or fuse, provided the fault current is high enough.
