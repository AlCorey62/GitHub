Why do LEDs trip RCDs?

26 October 20263 min read

Key points

  • LED drivers and electronic power supplies constantly leak a small current to earth: on the same RCD, these leakage currents add up.
  • A 30 mA RCD may trip from 15 mA: its standard allows tripping anywhere between half and all of its rated sensitivity.
  • Removing the protection is never the answer: spread the equipment over several circuits, each with its own RCD.

Picture this: one more bar of LED fixtures on a line that is already well loaded, and everything goes dark. No fixture is faulty, the power box is in good condition, the total load is far below the rating. Yet the 30 mA RCD has tripped. It is not broken: it is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Leakage currents add up

LED drivers, like most electronic power supplies (computers, screens, consoles, amplifiers), include a filter against electromagnetic interference. This filter connects the conductors to earth through capacitors: a small current flows to earth all the time, even when the equipment works perfectly.

For one device, this current stays low: the equipment safety standard (IEC 62368-1) limits it to 3.5 mA for plug-connected equipment. But an RCD does not see devices one by one: it measures the sum of all leakage downstream. Twenty devices leaking 1 mA each make 20 mA of permanent leakage on the same RCD.

A 30 mA RCD trips before 30 mA

A 30 mA RCD does not cut off at exactly 30 mA. Its product standards (IEC 61008 and IEC 61009) require it to trip at 30 mA at the latest, and allow it to trip from half that value, 15 mA. With 20 mA of permanent leakage, very little is needed to cross the threshold: one more device, a switch-on, some moisture on a connection.

At switch-on: inrush current

When powered up, each LED driver charges its input capacitors and draws, for a very brief moment, a current far higher than its operating current. Switched on all at once, dozens of drivers can trip the circuit breaker, even though the operating load is well within limits. RCD or breaker, each has its own job: we explain it in Why does it “trip”?

What we do

  • Split electronic equipment over several circuits, each protected by its own RCD: leakage is shared instead of adding up.
  • Keep a margin: protection manufacturers recommend keeping permanent leakage to about a third of the rated sensitivity, around ten milliamps on a 30 mA RCD.
  • Choose the right protection: for large amounts of electronics, RCDs with enhanced immunity, designed to withstand this leakage and inrush current better.
  • Stagger switch-on rather than powering everything up at once.

Removing the RCD, or replacing it with a less sensitive one, is never the answer: the 30 mA device is there to protect people.

An RCD that trips with LEDs is not faulty: it is adding up leakage. The answer is to split the circuits.
