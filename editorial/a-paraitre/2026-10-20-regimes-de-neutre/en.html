TT, TN, IT: earthing systems made simple

20 October 20264 min read

Key points

  • The earthing system describes how the source neutral and the metal casings of appliances are connected to earth.
  • It decides what trips when a fault occurs: the RCD in TT, the circuit breaker or fuse in TN. In IT, the first fault does not normally cause a trip: an alarm warns you, and it must be repaired quickly.
  • In temporary distribution with flexible cables, the neutral (N) and the protective conductor (PE) stay separate: no PEN conductor.

Electrical equipment often has a metal casing, which electricians call an exposed conductive part. If a live wire touches it, because of damaged insulation for example, the casing becomes live: this is an insulation fault, and anyone who touches it risks an electric shock. The earthing system describes the path the fault current then takes, and therefore which protective device will trip.

Two letters say it all

In French, it is called the “régime de neutre”. It is written with two letters:

  • The first says how the neutral of the source, a transformer or a generator, is connected to earth: T, connected directly to earth; I, isolated from earth or connected through a high impedance.
  • The second says how the metal casings of appliances are connected: T, to an earth electrode; N, to the source neutral, through the protective conductor, the green and yellow wire.

TT: the earth carries the current back, the RCD trips

The source neutral is connected to one earth electrode, the casings of appliances to another. When a fault occurs, the current flows through the ground to return to the source. Earth electrodes have a resistance that limits this current: it is usually too small to trip a circuit breaker or a fuse within the required time. Too small for the breaker does not mean harmless to a person: it is an RCD, together with the earthing of the metal casings, that detects the leakage and trips.

In France, this is the system used for installations connected to the public low-voltage network.

TN: the fault becomes a short circuit, the breaker trips

The metal casings are connected to the source neutral by the protective conductor. A direct fault then becomes a short circuit between line and neutral: the current is high, and the circuit breaker or fuse trips within the maximum time set by the standard.

On one condition: the current must be high enough. It depends on the source and on the length and cross-section of the cables: this is checked by calculation, then by measurement. If the condition is not met, the cables or the protective devices are adapted. In TN-S, an RCD can also be added; not in TN-C, where an RCD cannot be installed on the PEN.

A third letter specifies the TN system:

  • TN-S: the neutral and the protective conductor are two separate wires.
  • TN-C: they are combined in a single conductor, the PEN. It is only allowed in fixed installations, from 10 mm² in copper or 16 mm² in aluminium, and it is forbidden in flexible cables and in locations with a fire or explosion risk.

IT: on the first fault, normally nothing trips

The source neutral is isolated from earth, or connected through a high impedance. On the first fault, the current can only return to the source through the insulation and capacitance of the network to earth, and through that possible impedance. It normally stays very small: if the network meets the specified protection conditions, the first fault does not require a trip and the installation keeps running. The larger the network, the higher this current: these conditions are checked at the design stage.

An insulation monitoring device (IMD) then raises an alarm. The fault must be located and cleared quickly: if a second fault appears on another live conductor while the first one persists, the protective devices disconnect the circuits concerned. IT is found where an interruption would be serious, such as hospital operating theatres or continuous-process industries.

And at an event?

When it is plugged into a venue's installation, an event inherits its earthing system: usually TT if the venue is connected to the public low-voltage network in France, or the system chosen for its installation if it has its own transformer substation.

When it is powered by a generator, the event has its own source. The earthing system, the earthing arrangements and the protective devices are then decided and carried out on site, according to the generator and the installation it supplies, and must form a consistent whole.

In temporary distribution with flexible cables, the neutral (N) and the protective conductor (PE) stay separate: these cables cannot be used as a PEN. The only connection between neutral and PE is the one provided by the chosen earthing system, at the source; there is never one in downstream equipment.

TT, TN, IT: ask yourself what trips. The RCD, the circuit breaker, or, on the first fault in IT, nothing at all: it is the alarm that warns you.
