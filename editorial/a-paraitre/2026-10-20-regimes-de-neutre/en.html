TT, TN, IT: earthing systems made simple

20 October 20264 min read

Key points

  • The earthing system describes how the source neutral and the metal casings of appliances are connected to earth.
  • It decides what trips when a fault occurs: the RCD in TT, the circuit breaker or fuse in TN. In IT, nothing trips on the first fault and an alarm warns you.
  • At an event, neutral and earth stay separate all the way: a flexible cable cannot be used as a PEN conductor.

Electrical equipment often has a metal casing, which electricians call an exposed conductive part. If a live wire touches it, because of damaged insulation for example, the casing becomes live: this is an insulation fault, and anyone who touches it risks an electric shock. The earthing system describes the path the fault current then takes, and therefore which protective device will trip.

Two letters say it all

In French, it is called the “régime de neutre”. It is written with two letters:

  • The first says how the neutral of the source, a transformer or a generator, is connected to earth: T, connected directly to earth; I, isolated from earth or connected through a high impedance.
  • The second says how the metal casings of appliances are connected: T, to an earth electrode; N, to the source neutral, through the protective conductor, the green and yellow wire.

TT: the earth carries the current back, the RCD trips

The source neutral is connected to one earth electrode, the casings of appliances to another. When a fault occurs, the current flows through the ground to return to the source. Earth electrodes have a resistance that limits this current: it is usually far too small to trip a circuit breaker or a fuse. So it is an RCD that detects the leakage and trips.

In France, this is the system used for installations connected to the public low-voltage network.

TN: the fault becomes a short circuit, the breaker trips

The metal casings are connected to the source neutral by the protective conductor. A fault then becomes a short circuit between line and neutral: the current is high, and the circuit breaker or fuse trips within the maximum time set by the standard.

On one condition: the current must be high enough. On a very long cable, it no longer is. Cable lengths are therefore checked by calculation, and if needed an RCD is added.

A third letter specifies the TN system:

  • TN-S: the neutral and the protective conductor are two separate wires.
  • TN-C: they are combined in a single conductor, the PEN. It is only allowed in fixed installations, from 10 mm² in copper or 16 mm² in aluminium, and it is forbidden in flexible cables and in locations with a fire or explosion risk.

IT: on the first fault, nothing trips

The source neutral is isolated from earth, or connected through a high impedance. On the first fault, the current has almost no return path: it stays very small, the touch voltage is not dangerous, and the installation keeps running.

An insulation monitoring device (IMD) reports this fault. It must then be located and repaired quickly: a second fault, on another conductor, does cause a trip. IT is found where an interruption would be serious, such as hospital operating theatres or continuous-process industries.

And at an event?

When it is plugged into a venue's installation, an event inherits its earthing system: usually TT if the venue is connected to the public low-voltage network in France, or the system chosen for its installation if it has its own transformer substation.

When it is powered by a generator, the event has its own source. Connecting the alternator neutral to earth, driving the earth rods and choosing the protective devices are then done on site, and must form a consistent whole. For generators, the French construction-industry prevention body (OPPBTP) recommends connecting the alternator neutral to an earth electrode, in a TT or TN-S system, with 30 mA RCDs on the circuits.

Finally, at an event, everything is connected with flexible cables. As a flexible cable cannot be used as a PEN, neutral and earth stay separate all the way.

TT, TN, IT: ask yourself what trips. The RCD, the circuit breaker, or nothing at all, and then it is the alarm that warns you.
